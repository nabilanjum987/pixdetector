The problem: your photos get reused without you

Photographers, sellers and creators all hit the same moment: a portfolio shot, a product photo or a client proof turns up on someone else's listing or feed, with no credit and no payment. The usual advice is to watermark — but the complaints in photography forums are consistent. A small mark tucked in a corner is cropped out in seconds. A huge opaque mark ruins the photo for the honest client who was supposed to enjoy it. And the tools people try first either stamp their own watermark on top, cap the free exports, or demand an account before the first download. What people actually want is simple: their own name or logo, placed where it cannot be cropped away, at an opacity that reads clearly without wrecking the image — and a download that is theirs, free.

A watermark is a visible mark — text or a logo — drawn onto an image to show who made it or owns it. It does not stop copying on its own: it is a deterrent and a credit line. A single corner mark is easy to crop out; a tiled mark across the whole image is not, which is why stock-photo sites tile their previews.

Where should a watermark go?

Put some part of the mark on your subject, not in empty space beside it — a mark floating in a corner is cropped out in one click, while a mark touching the subject is hard to clone away without damaging the photo. For client proofs and pre-payment previews, use tiled mode: the text repeats diagonally across the whole image, the pattern stock sites use, and no clean area is left to cut. Keep the opacity in the range that fits the job — 20–40% for branding, up to 50–70% for client proofs (see the table below) — low enough that the photo still reads clearly, high enough that nobody can reuse it without the mark showing. A logo at low opacity looks the most professional; plain text is fine for quick proofs and internal drafts.

What a watermark can and cannot do

A watermark deters casual reuse and gives your work a credit line if it is shared — a stolen watermarked image still carries your name, which is free advertising of a sort. It does not prove ownership in court on its own, and it does not stop a determined thief: marks can be cloned out and images can be re-shot. For real protection, pair the watermark with the invisible evidence: embed your name and copyright in the image metadata, keep the high-resolution original private and share only a smaller watermarked version, and register valuable work with your country's copyright office if you want to claim damages.

Using a logo? Use a transparent PNG

The most common beginner mistake is uploading a JPG logo: it stamps a visible white rectangle onto the photo. Use a PNG with a real transparent background — the transparency is preserved and the logo sits cleanly on the image. If your logo has a white box behind it in the preview, that is the file, not the tool: re-export the logo as a transparent PNG and upload it again.

FAQ

Is this watermark tool really free?

Yes. Watermark as many images as you like — no signup, no export cap, and no watermark of ours added on top. Everything runs in your browser.

Does a watermark stop people stealing my photos?

No — honestly, nothing does. A watermark is a deterrent: it stops casual reuse and keeps your name on the image if it is shared. A corner mark can be cropped out; a tiled mark is much harder to remove.

What opacity should I use for a watermark?

20–40% for subtle branding; 50–70% tiled for client proofs; 40–60% tiled for stock previews. There is no single correct value — preview at the size the image will actually be displayed.

Text or logo — which watermark is better?

A logo at low opacity looks most professional and is harder to fake. Text works fine for quick proofs, drafts and a copyright notice. Use your logo for anything public.

Can I tile the watermark across the whole image?

Yes — switch on tiled mode and the text repeats diagonally across the entire photo, like stock-photo previews. That is the strongest protection this tool offers.

Is my image uploaded to a server?

No. The watermark is drawn onto your image in your browser with the canvas element. Nothing is uploaded, stored or shared.

Where should I place a watermark?

Bottom-right is the most common spot for branding and least distracting; centre or tiled across the frame is hardest to crop out. Avoid corners that social-media interface elements cover — and put some part of the mark on your subject, not in empty space beside it.

Can I add a logo as a watermark?

Yes — upload a transparent PNG logo; the transparency is preserved. A JPG logo will stamp a white box onto the photo. In this tool the logo replaces the text watermark — pick one or the other for each image.

Will watermarking reduce image quality?

No — the watermark is composited on top of the original pixels. The image is re-saved in the format you choose, so the file size can differ from the original; pick PNG for lossless quality.

Can I watermark many images at once?

Not on this page — it watermarks one image at a time. Batch tools apply the same settings to many images and hand back a ZIP; settle your settings on one image here, then use a batch tool for a set of twenty or more.

Can I add text and a logo to the same image?

Not on this tool — uploading a logo replaces the text watermark. Multi-layer tools let you add a logo plus a name plus a URL and position each separately; here, choose the one mark that matters most for the image.

What should the watermark say?

Your name, brand, website, or a copyright line such as “© Your Name”. Keep it short — a name or domain reads at small sizes; a full copyright sentence does not.

Should I watermark photos I post on social media?

It depends on the goal: for branding and attribution, a small corner mark; for proofs or pre-payment previews, a tiled mark. Many photographers watermark social posts but show clean images on their own website.

Can a watermark be removed?

Yes — with Photoshop’s Content-Aware fill, free AI removers or phone apps, often in seconds. That is why this page never promises theft-proofing: a watermark is friction and a credit line, not a lock. A tiled mark across the whole frame is the hardest to remove cleanly.

Is removing someone else’s watermark legal?

Removing a watermark from someone else’s copyrighted image without authorisation may constitute copyright infringement. Commenters on removal tutorials routinely call it theft — this tool only adds watermarks, it never removes them.
