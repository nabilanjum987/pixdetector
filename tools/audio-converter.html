The problem: the file you have is not the file you need

Audio incompatibility is a small problem that arrives at the worst moment. An iPhone voice memo is an M4A, and plenty of Windows apps, car stereos and older podcast hosts still only accept MP3. A WhatsApp voice note is an OPUS file, and Apple Music, Voice Memos, iMessage and CarPlay all refuse it outright — Apple has never shipped system-wide Opus support. A recording booth exports WAV, a field recorder saves FLAC, and the app you need to upload to wants MP3. People search for a free audio converter because they want one file changed, fast, without installing software, creating an account, or handing a private recording to a random server — and they want to know the converted file will actually play where they need it.

An audio converter is a tool that decodes a sound file from one format and re-encodes it in another. Audio formats fall into two families: lossless formats (WAV, FLAC) keep every sample, and lossy formats (MP3, M4A/AAC, OGG, OPUS) discard data the ear barely notices to shrink the file to about a tenth of its size. Converting between them changes the container and the encoding, never the performance itself — and converting lossy to lossy always loses a little more detail, so you should convert from the original file whenever you can.

Why your iPhone audio is not MP3 — and what to do about it

Apple picked its own formats: Voice Memos exports M4A (AAC in an MP4 container), and messaging apps save voice notes as OPUS. Neither is a bug — but both break when the next app in line only accepts MP3. The fix is a one-way conversion to MP3 at 128–192 kbps for speech, or to M4A if the destination is Apple-only. For a WhatsApp voice note, convert OPUS to M4A at 64–96 kbps mono: the note was recorded around 24 kbps, so picking 320 kbps only makes a bigger file with no better sound.

Bitrates, honestly

96 kbps is fine for voice and the smallest file. 128 kbps is the standard for speech and most music. 192 kbps sounds better on good headphones. 320 kbps is the biggest file and only worth it for music from a high-quality source — it cannot rescue a low-quality recording. WAV and FLAC have no bitrate setting because they are lossless: WAV stores every sample (about 10 MB per minute), FLAC stores the same audio losslessly at roughly half that size.

FAQ

Is this audio converter really free?

Yes. Convert as many files as you like — no watermark, no sign-up, no upload and no ads. The first conversion downloads a ~30 MB engine into your browser, cached after that.

Why won't my OPUS voice note play on my iPhone?

Apple devices do not support OPUS files in Music, Voice Memos, Files, iMessage or CarPlay. Convert the OPUS file to M4A (for Apple) or MP3 (for everywhere) here and it will play.

How do I convert M4A to MP3?

Upload the M4A, pick MP3 as the output and a bitrate (192 kbps is a good balance), then press Convert. The MP3 plays on virtually any device or app.

Will I lose quality converting between formats?

Converting lossy to lossy (for example MP3 to M4A, or OPUS to MP3) loses a little detail each time, because both formats discard data. Convert from the original file when you can, and match the source bitrate rather than picking the highest number.

Why does the first conversion take so long?

The first conversion downloads a ~30 MB conversion engine (ffmpeg.wasm) into your browser. It is cached after that, so later conversions start fast. Conversion is also slower on big files and on phones.

Is my audio uploaded anywhere?

No. Your file never leaves your device — it is read, converted and downloaded entirely in your browser. Nothing is sent to a server, and nothing is stored after you close the page.
