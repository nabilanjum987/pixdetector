The problem: no dice on the table, and nobody trusts a hidden roll

Board game night without the dice box, a D&D session over video call, a classroom probability lesson — the moment someone needs a die that is not there, the search starts. The complaints about online dice rollers are not about the maths. They are about trust and clutter: players suspect the roller is rigged after a bad streak, the tool is buried in ads, it only rolls one d6, or it hides the individual dice and shows only a total. On forums for virtual tabletops, the same thread comes up every few months — "the rolls are either super high or super low" — and the answer is always the same: that is what random looks like in a small sample. What players actually want is a roller that shows every die, adds the total honestly, and says plainly how the numbers are drawn.

How do I roll dice online?

Pick a die — d4, d6, d8, d10, d12 or d20 — choose how many dice to roll (1 to 6), add a modifier if your game uses one, and press ROLL DICE. Each die is shown separately, then added up with your modifier into one total. It is free, with no signup and no app to install, and every roll is drawn with your browser's secure random source, so every face of every die has exactly the same chance.

A dice roller is a virtual replacement for physical dice: it picks a number from 1 to the number of sides, once per die, and adds the results. A d20 is the twenty-sided die used in Dungeons & Dragons; a d6 is the ordinary six-sided die from board games. Rolling 2d6+3 means roll two six-sided dice, add them, then add 3.

How to roll dice in 3 steps

Step 1: choose your die type — d6 for board games, d20 for D&D. Step 2: set how many dice (up to 6) and a modifier, such as +3 for a strength bonus or -2 for a penalty. Step 3: press ROLL DICE (or hit Space) and read each die plus the total. Your last 10 rolls stay in the history below.

Is this dice roller really random — or rigged?

It is random, and the streaks are normal. Each die is drawn independently with crypto.getRandomValues, the browser's secure random source, so a roll has no memory of the last one. A run of low numbers never makes a high number "due" — that feeling is the gambler's fallacy, and it is why bad streaks feel rigged when they are not. In fact, computer dice are fairer than most physical dice: real dice can be slightly weighted or unevenly shaped, while a good digital draw gives every face exactly equal odds. If your whole group rolls at the same moment and gets similar results, that is coincidence in a small sample, not a shared seed.

What do the colours mean?

On a d20, a natural 20 — the highest face — is shown in green: a critical hit in D&D. A natural 1 is shown in red: a critical miss. The colours only flag standout dice; they never change the total. The modifier is added after the dice are summed, exactly as the rules of most tabletop games require.
