The problem: "resize without losing quality"

"Resize without losing quality" is the phrase people type most — and it is also the phrase that causes the most disappointment. Resized images go blurry or pixelated, usually when a small image is enlarged. The second frustration is simpler: people do not know the numbers. Instagram wants 1080×1080, a YouTube thumbnail wants 1280×720, a passport photo wants 600×600 — and guessing wrong means an upload rejected or a cropped, stretched picture. The third is aspect-ratio distortion: change the width without the height and faces go wide. People want the exact pixel size for a named platform, in one click, with no quality loss and no upload.

It is free, with no signup and no upload, because the resize runs in your browser. Scaling down keeps your image sharp; scaling up will always soften it, because no tool can invent detail that was never in the file.

An image resizer is a tool that changes an image's pixel dimensions — its width and height — by resampling it onto a new grid of pixels. When you scale down, it averages nearby pixels together, which keeps the picture sharp. When you scale up, it has to invent pixels between the real ones, which is why enlargements look soft or blurry. The aspect ratio is the width-to-height proportion: change one side without the other and the picture stretches.

What size do the platforms actually want?

Instagram square post: 1080×1080. Instagram portrait: 1080×1350. YouTube thumbnail: 1280×720. Facebook / Open Graph link image: 1200×630. X post image: 1600×900. Passport-style photo: 600×600. HD video frame: 1920×1080. If the platform crops your image anyway, it is because your ratio does not match — resizing to the exact numbers here avoids that crop, and the aspect-ratio lock keeps faces and logos from stretching while you do it.

Which format, and what quality?

Use JPG for photos: it is the smallest file, and the quality slider lets you trade a little sharpness for a much smaller file — 85–90% looks identical to most eyes. Use PNG for graphics, logos, screenshots and anything needing transparency, but expect bigger files. Use WebP for websites: it is smaller than both for the same quality on modern browsers. If your resized image will be uploaded to a platform that recompresses it anyway, download at 90% quality — going higher only makes a bigger file the platform will shrink again.

FAQ

Is this image resizer really free?

Yes. Resize as many images as you like, free, with no signup, no watermark and no upload — everything runs in your browser.

Can I resize an image without losing quality?

Only when scaling down. Making an image smaller keeps it sharp. Enlarging a small image cannot create detail that was never there, so it will always soften — no tool can resize up without some quality loss.

Why did my resized image look stretched?

It was stretched because the aspect ratio was not locked: the width and height were changed by different amounts. Keep the lock on, change one side, and let the other follow — or crop the image to the target ratio first.

What size should an Instagram post be?

A square Instagram post is 1080 by 1080 pixels. A YouTube thumbnail is 1280 by 720. A Facebook link preview (Open Graph) image is 1200 by 630. This tool has all three as one-click presets.

Is my image uploaded to a server?

No. Your image is resized in your browser with the canvas element. Nothing is uploaded, stored, or shared.

Which format should I download — PNG, JPG or WebP?

Use JPG for photos (smallest files, with a quality slider), PNG for graphics, logos and anything needing transparency, and WebP for the smallest files on modern websites.
