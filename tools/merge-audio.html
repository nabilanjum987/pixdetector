The problem: your audio is in pieces, and the easy fixes are broken

People end up with audio in pieces all the time: a podcast recorded as intro, main segment and outro; an audiobook split into chapters; voice notes that belong in one file. The fixes people actually try are the problem. The old Windows copy /b trick glues MP3 files together byte-for-byte, and on variable-bitrate files the result reports the wrong length — Windows shows only the first clip's duration and the player's progress bar no longer matches what you hear. Audacity does the job properly, but it is a full editor you have to download, install and learn for a one-minute task. And phones have no built-in way to join audio files at all. What people want is simple: drop the clips in, check the order, get one file back — without installing anything, creating an account, or uploading private recordings to a stranger's server.

How do I merge audio files for free?

Upload two or more audio files here, arrange them in order with the up and down arrows, pick a gap between clips if you want one, and press Merge audio. The files are decoded and joined in your browser into one track, which you can preview and download as a WAV file — free, with no signup and no upload.

Merging audio means joining separate sound files end to end so they play as one continuous track: the second clip starts where the first ends. It is different from mixing, where two tracks play at the same time. A proper merge decodes every file to raw audio first, lines the clips up at a single sample rate, and renders one new file — which is why the merged track reports its true length, unlike a byte-for-byte join.

How to merge audio in 3 steps

Step 1: add two or more audio files — MP3, WAV, M4A, OGG, FLAC or OPUS (WhatsApp voice notes), mixed formats welcome; each file is decoded in your browser and never uploaded. Step 2: arrange the clips in playback order with the arrows, then pick a preset for your job (podcast, audiobook, WhatsApp voice notes, lecture, mixtape) or set the crossfade, silence gap and match-loudness controls yourself. Step 3: press Merge audio, preview the result in the player, then download the merged WAV file.

Hear a click at the join? It is probably the sample rate

The most common complaint about merged audio is a click or pop at every seam, and it is usually not your fault: the clips were recorded at different sample rates — 44.1 kHz vs 48 kHz — and joined without resampling, or MP3 encoder padding added about 50 ms of silence at the start of each file. This tool resamples everything to one rate before joining, so the rates cannot clash. If a pop survives, a 10 ms crossfade at the join fixes it — set the crossfade slider to 0.5 s and the seam disappears. That is also why the merged file reports its true length: decoding and re-rendering beats gluing MP3 files together byte-for-byte, which is what makes players show the wrong duration.

Your tags will not survive the merge — plan for it

Most online joiners treat audio as raw blocks and discard title, author, genre and cover art during the merge — an organised audiobook loses its chapters' tags. This page is no exception: its WAV export carries no ID3 tags. If metadata matters to you, download the free tag editor Mp3tag after merging and re-add title, author and cover art in a minute. Knowing before you merge beats discovering it after.

Why the merged file is a WAV, not an MP3

Browsers can decode almost any audio format, but they cannot encode MP3 — there is no built-in MP3 encoder in Chrome, Firefox, Edge or Safari. So the honest export here is WAV: uncompressed, full quality, playable everywhere, at about 10 MB per minute. If your browser ever gains MP3 encoding, the MP3 button on this page lights up automatically. If you need a small MP3 now, download the WAV and run it through our free audio converter.

What to merge, and what not to

End-to-end merging is right for podcast segments, audiobook chapters, split recordings, voice notes in order, and turning a playlist of clips into one file for a car stereo or a slideshow. It is the wrong tool for a voiceover over music, a song mashup, or anything where two sounds must play together — that is mixing, and a multi-track editor like Audacity is the right tool for it.

FAQ

How do I merge audio files online for free?

Drop two or more files into a free browser joiner, drag them into order, add a crossfade if you want one, and export — no account needed on the in-browser tools.

Can I merge MP3 files into one MP3?

Yes — drop the MP3s in, check the order, export as MP3. The join re-encodes once; at 192 kbps or above the extra generation is inaudible. This page exports WAV by default, because browsers cannot encode MP3 — convert the WAV to MP3 with our audio converter if you need a small file.

Does merging audio reduce quality?

Joining MP3s adds one re-encode generation; joining WAV files is mathematically lossless (samples concatenated bit-for-bit). Export as WAV or FLAC if you want an untouched master.

Why is there a click between my merged tracks?

Usually mismatched sample rates (44.1 vs 48 kHz) or MP3 encoder padding (about 50 ms of silence at each file’s start). Resample everything to one rate first, or apply a 10 ms crossfade at the join. This tool resamples everything to one rate before joining.

How do I merge WhatsApp voice notes into one file?

Download the voice notes to your computer (they are OPUS files), then upload them to an audio joiner that accepts OPUS/M4A, arrange in order and export as MP3. This page accepts OPUS and M4A directly.

Can I merge audio files on my iPhone?

Yes in a mobile browser; there is no built-in iPhone feature — the GarageBand workaround is transfer voice memos to the GarageBand folder, import, align, export as M4A. Keep the total length modest on a phone, as phones have less memory.

How do I add an intro and outro to my podcast?

Add intro jingle, main segment, outro in that order, set a short crossfade (2–3 s) so the seams blend, export as MP3 for your host or WAV for archival. The podcast preset on this page sets that up in one tap.

Can I merge an audiobook recorded in chapters?

Yes — drop each chapter in end to end, tick “match loudness” if chapters were recorded on different days at different volumes, export as MP3 or FLAC. One Audacity forum user merged audiobooks with 99 tracks per disc.

What is the difference between merging and mixing or overlapping audio?

Merging (joining) plays clips end to end, one after another. Overlapping/mixing plays them at the same time — voiceover over background music. They are different jobs; use the Merge vs overlap chooser on this page.

Will my merged file keep its song titles and cover art?

Usually not with online joiners — most strip ID3 tags during the merge. If metadata matters, re-tag afterwards with a free tag editor like Mp3tag. This page warns you before you merge, and its WAV export carries no tags.

How many audio files can I merge at once?

There is no export-per-day cap here and no track-count cap — the practical limit is your device’s memory. For comparison, cut.audio free allows up to 30 tracks of up to an hour each with 10 exports a day, and NVR Nexus allows 30 tracks / 200 MB on desktop. Keep very long merges on a desktop.

Can I merge audio files of different formats?

Yes — browser joiners decode every input to raw audio first, so MP3 + WAV + FLAC + OGG mix freely; everything is resampled to a common rate before joining.

Is it safe to merge private recordings online?

Only with an in-browser tool — the audio never leaves your device. Server-side tools upload your files (Aspose deletes them after 24 hours, per its own page); for interviews, meetings or legal recordings, use a local tool.

Do I need to install Audacity to merge audio?

No — for a simple end-to-end join, a browser joiner does it in seconds. Audacity is the free answer when you need precise editing, fades per clip, or tag preservation.

Can I crossfade between songs when merging?

Yes — set the crossfade slider (1–3 s is typical); it applies at every seam so each clip fades out as the next fades in. Leave it at zero for clean hard cuts.
