The problem: no tape measure, and the screen lies

It happens constantly: you need to measure something small — a ring, a screw, a photo print, the width of a phone case, a gap on a shelf — and there is no ruler in the drawer. So you search for an online ruler, and most of them hand you a picture of a ruler that is simply wrong, because every screen packs a different number of pixels into an inch. A 27-inch 4K monitor holds about 163 pixels per inch; a phone can hold 460. A ruler that assumes the web default of 96 pixels per inch can be off by 60% on a modern phone, and nothing on the page tells you. On social media the same frustration shows up around heavily promoted "AR ruler" apps — one widely shared reel's comments report an installed app that did nothing but show ads. What people actually want is a plain screen ruler that admits the problem and fixes it: calibrate once against a real object, then measure.

How do I measure something with an online ruler?

Calibrate once with a standard credit card, then hold the object against your screen and read its length in cm, mm or inches — free, no signup, no app. Without calibration the ruler guesses your screen's pixel density and can be far out, so this page badges every uncalibrated reading ESTIMATE until you set it. Calibrated accuracy is about 0.5–1 mm over 10 cm; uncalibrated, expect 10–20% error or worse.

An online ruler is a measuring tool drawn on your screen at real physical size. Screens do not know their own physical size, so a browser measures everything in CSS pixels — and a CSS pixel is not a fixed physical length. Calibration tells the ruler how many CSS pixels make one real inch on your screen, using a physical reference you already own: a standard card, which is 85.60 mm wide by international standard ISO 7810.

How to use the online ruler in 3 steps

Step 1 — calibrate: hold a standard credit or ID card against the blue box and drag the slider until the box is exactly as wide as your card. The page now knows your screen's real pixel density. Step 2 — choose a unit: centimetres, millimetres or inches, and horizontal or vertical. Step 3 — measure: lay the object flat against the screen along the ruler, or click two points on the ruler to measure the distance between them.

How accurate is it, exactly?

Calibrated, expect about 0.5–1 mm over 10 cm for household use — that is the figure the honest ruler pages state, and it is what a careful card calibration delivers on a flat screen with a straight-on view. Uncalibrated, a desktop monitor is typically 10–20% off and a phone far worse, because the browser's 96 PPI guess is nowhere near a 300–460 PPI phone screen. Two things undo a good calibration without warning: browser zoom (if Chrome or Safari is set to 110%, your ruler is a lie) and moving the window to a different monitor. This page watches for both and warns you when it detects them — recalibrate when it does.

What it can't do: a screen ruler cannot measure curves, circumferences or room dimensions, anything longer than your screen, or anything that does not lie flat against it. For 0.1 mm work — engineering fits, machined parts — use calipers, not a screen. For a ring, a calibrated reading is the minimum; an uncalibrated one is not good enough.

Measure without a ruler — your hand is one

No ruler, no screen, no card? Your own hand is a rough measuring tool — a viral infographic shared across Facebook groups lists six hand positions: index finger ≈ 1 inch, thumb width ≈ 2 inches, fist ≈ 4 inches, thumb-to-index span ≈ 6 inches, thumb-to-pinky span ≈ 9 inches, elbow-to-fingertip ≈ 18 inches. These are estimates — hands differ — so measure your own hand against a card once, then it is a usable rough ruler anywhere.

What you can — and cannot — measure

You can measure flat, small objects held against the screen: paper, cards, photos, jewellery, screws, phone cases, printed diagrams. You cannot measure a room, a person, or anything that does not fit on your screen — for that you need a tape measure, not a web page. And a screen measurement is never as precise as a steel ruler: after calibration, expect accuracy within about half a millimetre for small objects, which is plenty for everyday checks but not for engineering.

FAQ

Is an online ruler accurate?

Only after calibration. Calibrated, expect about 0.5–1 mm over 10 cm for household use. Uncalibrated, a desktop monitor can be 10–20% off, and a phone far worse. Do not trust an uncalibrated reading at all.

How do I calibrate an online ruler?

Hold a standard bank or credit card (85.6 mm wide) flat against the screen and drag the on-screen box or slider until its width matches the card exactly, then confirm. No card handy: type your screen’s diagonal in inches instead.

Why is my online ruler the wrong size?

Because every screen has a different pixel density and the browser assumes 96 pixels per inch. A 4K monitor and a 1080p laptop show the same 100-pixel line at different physical sizes — calibration is what fixes it.

Do I need to calibrate every time?

No — calibration is saved in your browser’s local storage and persists until you clear browser data, switch screens or monitors, or change display scaling.

Does browser zoom affect an online ruler?

Yes — zooming in or out stretches or shrinks the ruler along with everything else on the page, so calibrate and measure at 100% zoom. This page warns you if it detects zoom that is not 100%.

Can I use an online ruler on my phone?

Yes — on a phone held upright the ruler runs down the long side of the screen. Accuracy is best on iPhones (recognised exactly); Android is estimated, usually within a few percent.

What is PPI and why does it matter?

PPI (pixels per inch) is how densely a screen packs pixels. The ruler needs it to convert pixel positions into real centimetres or inches — a 227 PPI MacBook Retina needs far more pixels per centimetre than a 96 PPI laptop screen.

Can I measure in inches instead of cm?

Yes — switch units in the toolbar; 1 inch = 25.4 mm exactly, and a correct centimetre calibration makes inches correct too.

How do I measure something longer than my screen?

You can’t in one placement — measure in sections, or use the scroll-to-extend mode. For anything much longer, use a tape measure; a screen ruler is for small objects.

Can an online ruler measure a ring size?

Yes, if it is calibrated — a ring is the one household case where a fraction of a millimetre matters, and a sizer built on the same card calibration is the honest way to offer it. A rough uncalibrated reading is not good enough for a ring.

Is it safe and private to use an online ruler?

The ruler itself needs no upload — it is drawn in your browser from your screen’s pixel density, and calibration stays in localStorage on your device. Avoid tools that ask you to upload a photo to measure it.

Do I need to download an app?

No — the browser tools work with nothing to install; that is the point. App-store ruler apps exist, but at least one heavily promoted AR ruler app drew a comment that it was “non-functional beyond ads” after install.

Can I print an online ruler?

Yes, but print at “Actual Size” / 100% scale — “Fit to Page” shrinks it by about 5–7% — and check the print against a credit card before using it.

What can I measure with an online ruler?

Small flat things held against the screen: screws, keys, coins, rings, phone cases, cables, beads, watch bands.

What is the most accurate online ruler?

There is no verified ranking — the honest answer is that the most accurate one is whichever asks you to calibrate with a card and tells you its error. Any ruler that never asks for calibration is an estimate wearing a ruler’s clothes.

Related questions

How do I measure 1 inch online? Switch to inches and calibrate first — after calibration, the "1" mark is exactly one real inch.

Can I measure in millimetres? Yes — switch to mm mode and every small tick is 1 mm, with longer marks at each centimetre.

Does the calibration save? Yes — your setting is saved in this browser, so it is still calibrated next visit. It resets if you clear site data.

Why does the ruler change when I zoom? Browser zoom changes the size of a CSS pixel, which breaks calibration. Keep zoom at 100% while measuring — this page warns you if it detects zoom or a screen change.

Can I measure in pixels? Yes — switch to Pixels (px) mode. It measures on-screen elements in CSS pixels, which is what designers and developers need; it does not need calibration, because a CSS pixel is the unit itself.

Does auto-detection work on Android? Not exactly — Android screens vary too much to recognise by size alone, so the page estimates them (usually within a few percent). iPhones, iPads and MacBooks are recognised exactly; anything else should use the card method.
