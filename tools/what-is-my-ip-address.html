Why people check their IP address

Most people only look up their IP when something is already wrong. A streaming service says "this video is not available in your region" even though you are at home. YouTube TV throws a geo-error. Your VPN is on, yet Google still shows your old city. Or your computer suddenly has no internet at all and the network settings show a strange address starting with 169.254. In each case the question is the same: is the problem my device, my internet provider, or my VPN? One instant answer — your public IP and where it appears to be — tells you which one to blame, and what to do next.

What is my IP address? The direct answer

Your public IP address is the address your internet provider assigns to your connection, and it is the address every website sees when you visit. It usually reveals only an approximate city or region and your provider's name — never your street address or your name. This page shows it instantly, free, with no signup.

An IP address is a number that identifies your connection on the internet, in the same way a phone number identifies a phone line. Most home connections get a new one from time to time, so the address you see today may change after a router restart. Your devices also have a second, private address (usually starting 192.168.x.x) that only works inside your home network — websites cannot see it, and no web page can show it to you.

What your IP reveals — and what it cannot reveal

An IP address can reveal your approximate area and your provider, which is why streaming services and websites use it to guess your region. It cannot reveal your exact home address, your name, or what you are doing online.

Why is my location wrong?

IP geolocation maps your address to your internet provider's routing hub — not to your house, and never by GPS. The country is usually reliable, but the city is only an estimate, and it can be off by a whole region. Mobile data, a VPN, or CGNAT (see below) can shift it further, because the address may be registered to a hub far from where you actually are. If the database entry behind your address is out of date, you can appear in a nearby city or even another region.

Nothing on this page can move your IP to the right city, because the location lives in a database owned by a geolocation provider, fed by records from your ISP. If the location is wrong, report it to your internet provider and ask them to update their records with the database owners — that is the only route that works, and it can take days or weeks. In the meantime, streaming geo-errors are usually fixed by the service itself once its own database updates, not by restarting your router. Restarting the router may give you a different IP, which sometimes lands in a correctly mapped range — but it is luck, not a fix.

A short note on CGNAT

Some providers use CGNAT (carrier-grade NAT): they share one public IP between many customers, so the WAN address your router shows is not a real public address. That is why the address in your router settings and the address on this page can differ — and why port-forwarding and home servers break under CGNAT: incoming connections simply cannot reach you. The fix is to ask your provider for a public or static IP, which is usually a paid option.

How to find your local (private) IP on any device

This page shows your public IP. Your local IP — the one your router gives your device — is found on the device itself:

Windows

Open Command Prompt and run ipconfig. Look for "IPv4 Address" under your Wi-Fi or Ethernet adapter.

Mac

Open System Settings → Network, select your network, then Details. Or run ifconfig in Terminal.

iPhone

Open Settings → Wi-Fi, tap the (i) next to your connected network — the IP is shown under "IP Address".

Android

Open Settings → Wi-Fi, tap your connected network (or the gear icon) — the IP is listed in the network details. Menu names vary by maker.

Linux

Run ip addr in a terminal, or hostname -I for a one-line answer.

Any device — public IP

Open this page in any browser. It shows your public IP instantly, on phone or computer.

Get your IP from the terminal (curl)

Developers and power users often skip the browser entirely. These one-liners print just the address — no page, no ads:

curl https://api.ipify.org # your public IPv4 address curl https://api64.ipify.org # your public IPv6 address (if supported) curl https://ipinfo.io/ip # another plain-text option

When the location is wrong, what actually fixes it?

A VPN hides your real IP by showing the VPN server's address instead, but it does not change the location databases, saved locations in your Google account, or GPS on your phone — which is why a VPN can be on and Google can still show your old city. And if your device shows a 169.254.x.x address, that is not a real internet address at all: it means your device could not reach the DHCP server that hands out addresses, so restart your router and device first, then check the cable or Wi-Fi, and call your provider if it keeps happening.

FAQ

What is my IP address?

Your public IP address is shown at the top of this page. It is the address your internet provider assigns to your connection, and it is the address every website sees when you visit. It can change when you restart your router or switch networks.

Public vs private — what's the difference?

A public IP identifies your whole connection to the internet; a private IP (like 192.168.x.x or 10.x.x.x) identifies one device inside your home network. Websites see only your public IP. Your private IP is set by your router and never leaves your home network.

Why does my network settings IP differ from this page?

Your device settings show your private IP, given to your device by your router. This page shows your public IP, given to your connection by your internet provider. They are different addresses for different jobs — both are correct at the same time.

Why is my location wrong?

IP location comes from a database that maps address ranges to places — usually your provider's routing hub, not your house. If the database is out of date, you can appear in a nearby city or another region. Only your internet provider can ask the database owners to correct it; no lookup tool can fix it.

Why does my IP keep changing?

Most home connections get a dynamic IP that changes when your router restarts or your provider reassigns it. IPv6 addresses often change daily by design (privacy extensions). If you need a fixed address, ask your provider — it is usually a paid option.

Why do I have two IP addresses (IPv4 + IPv6)?

IPv4 (like 203.0.113.45) is the older format; IPv6 (like 2001:db8::1) is the newer, longer format. Many connections support both at once, so you have one of each. This page shows both when your connection supports them.

Can someone find my exact address from my IP?

No. A public IP reveals only an approximate area — usually a city or region — plus your provider's name. It does not reveal your street address or your name. Only your internet provider holds the record linking an IP to a customer, and it does not share that publicly.

Can someone hack me if they know my IP?

Knowing an IP alone gives an attacker very little: it reveals an approximate area and your provider, not your files or passwords. The real risks are open ports and unpatched devices on your network, not the address itself. Keep your router firmware updated and use strong passwords.

How do I check if my VPN is working?

Note your IP on this page, then connect your VPN and check again. If the address and location change to the VPN server's, the VPN is working. If the address stays the same, the VPN is not routing your traffic.

Why does the site still show my real IP with VPN on?

A VPN can leak your real address through WebRTC, IPv6 or DNS, or your browser may still show a saved location. Run the WebRTC leak check on this page, and check your VPN settings for IPv6 and DNS leak protection.

What is CGNAT and why can't I host a server?

CGNAT (carrier-grade NAT) means your provider shares one public IP between many customers, so your router's WAN address is not a real public address. Incoming connections cannot reach you, which breaks port-forwarding and home servers. The fix is to ask your provider for a public or static IP, usually for a fee.

How do I find my IP on Windows/Mac/iPhone/Android?

For your public IP, open this page on any device — it shows it instantly. For your private (local) IP: on Windows run ipconfig in Command Prompt; on Mac open System Settings, then Network (or run ifconfig); on iPhone or Android open Wi-Fi settings and tap your connected network.

How do I hide/change my IP?

A VPN or proxy makes websites see a different public IP. Restarting your router often gives you a new dynamic IP. Tor also hides your IP. None of these makes you anonymous on its own — a VPN changes what your provider sees, and shifts some trust to the VPN provider.

Is it safe to give my IP to support?

Yes, for troubleshooting. Support teams ask for it to check your connection and location. An IP alone does not reveal your name, exact address or private files.

How do I get just the IP as plain text (curl)?

From a terminal, run curl https://api.ipify.org to print just your public IPv4 address, or curl https://api64.ipify.org for IPv6 when your connection supports it. These are the same free services this page uses.

Why do two different IP checkers show different results?

Because they can take different paths to you: one may show your IPv4 address while the other shows your IPv6 address, and they may use different location databases or see you through different network routes (VPN, mobile data, CGNAT). Both addresses are yours — they are just different views of the same connection.
