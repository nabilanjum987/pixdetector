The problem: "what time is it there, right now?"

It is the most ordinary question in a connected world — calling family overseas, joining a meeting with a team in three countries, checking whether the London office is still open — and the answers people get are oddly unhelpful: a single city on a search page, or a clock site buried in ads and pop-ups. What people actually want is several cities side by side, at a glance, with the date attached, so they can see at once that it is tomorrow in Sydney while it is still today in New York. On social media this need barely appears as discussion at all — nobody posts about world clocks, they just search for the time — which is itself the signal: the tool has to be instant, obvious, and free of friction. This page is that: the cities you care about, live, with nothing to install and no account standing between you and the answer.

A world clock is a tool that shows the current local time in cities around the world at the same moment. The Earth is divided into 24 time zones, each roughly one hour apart, measured as an offset from UTC — Coordinated Universal Time, the world's time standard. A city ahead of UTC, like Tokyo at UTC+9, is nine hours ahead of London in winter; a city behind it, like New York at UTC-5, is five hours behind.

How to use the world clock in 3 steps

Step 1: read the default cities — your own local time is shown first, then New York, London, Paris, Dubai, Mumbai, Singapore, Tokyo and Sydney. Step 2: type any city in the search box and tap Add city; tap × on a card to remove it. Step 3: switch between 12-hour and 24-hour time, and read the "ahead/behind" line under each clock to plan a call.

How time zones actually work

Every place on Earth keeps time as an offset from UTC. London sits at UTC+0 in winter and UTC+1 in summer (British Summer Time). New York is UTC-5 in winter and UTC-4 in summer. Tokyo never changes — UTC+9 all year. That is why the gap between two cities changes through the year: New York and London are 5 hours apart in winter but only 4 in summer, because their daylight-saving clocks change on different dates. This page never does that maths by hand — it asks your browser for each city's real offset right now, including daylight saving, so the times are right on any date.

FAQ

Is this world clock really free?

Yes. Free, no signup, no app, no limit — the clocks run entirely in your browser.

Is the time accurate?

It is as accurate as your device clock, because it takes the current moment from your device and converts it into each city's time zone. If your device clock is a minute fast, every clock here is a minute fast.

Does it handle daylight saving time?

Yes, automatically. Your browser's time-zone data knows each city's DST rules, so offsets change on the right dates without you doing anything.

How many cities can I add?

As many as you like from the list of major cities and time zones. The defaults cover the eight most-searched cities.

Does it need the internet?

Only to load the page. After that the clocks tick from your device clock, so they keep working offline.

Why is it tomorrow in Sydney but today in New York?

Because Sydney is about 14–16 hours ahead of New York. When it is morning in New York, it is already the next day's early hours in Sydney — the date line sits between them in the Pacific.

Related questions

How do I convert a meeting time between zones? Add both cities, read the "ahead/behind" line, and add or subtract that gap from your meeting time.

Why do some cities share the same time? Because they sit in the same time zone — for example Paris, Berlin and Madrid all use Central European Time.

Does the clock save my cities? Yes — your added cities are saved in this browser, so they are still here next visit.
